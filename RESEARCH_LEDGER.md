## SECTION 1: AJANTA CAVE PAINTINGS & THE ṢAḌDANTA JĀTAKA

### 1.1 Historical & Architectural Framework
- **Location & Geography:** Waghora River horseshoe gorge, Aurangabad district (Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar), Maharashtra, India.
- **Period & Patronage:**
  - Cave 17 belongs to the second phase of excavation (Vākāṭaka period), dated c. 460–477 CE under the reign of Hariṣeṇa (Vākāṭaka dynasty of Vatsagulma).
  - Cave 17 donor inscription: Inscribed on the left wall of the veranda in 29 verses of Sanskrit kavya, recording the excavation of the "monolithic, excellent cave" by King Upendragupta (also known as Rājā Upendragupta of Rṣīka/Khandesh), a feudatory subordinate of the Vākāṭaka monarch Hariṣeṇa.
  - Spink's chronology (*Ajanta: History and Development*, Vol. 1): Cave 17 was initiated around 466 CE and completed by 477 CE prior to the abrupt cessation following Hariṣeṇa's death.
- **Painting Technique & Plaster Chemistry:**
  - Not true Italian *fresco buono* (which is painted into wet lime plaster), but rather **tempera / fresco secco**.
  - Underlayer: Coarse mud plaster composed of local ferruginous clay, rock grit, sand, organic binder (rice husks, vegetable fibres), and cow dung applied directly onto the hammered basalt wall.
  - Intermediate/finishing ground: Thin layer (1–3 mm) of fine slaked lime plaster (*chunam*) or fine kaolin/gypsum slip, smoothed with an agate burnisher.
  - Pigments: Natural inorganic earth minerals:
    - Red & Yellow Ochres (*geru* and *ramraj*): Iron oxides from the Deccan trap rocks.
    - Green (*terra verde* / celadonite): Volcanic silicate minerals.
    - White: Gypsum, kaolin, or slaked conch-shell lime.
    - Black: Lampblack / soot collected from vegetable oil lamps.
    - Blue (*lapis lazuli* / ultramarine): Imported from the Badakhshan mines (modern north-eastern Afghanistan), testifying to extensive international trade networks in the 5th century CE. Used selectively for elite garments, celestial deities, and lotus pools.
  - Binder: Organic plant gum, animal glue, or egg tempera (debated by conservation chemists; recent ASI-NRLC studies confirm plant-gum water-soluble binders).

### 1.2 Primary Literary Sources for the Ṣaḍdanta Tale
- **Pali Canon:** *Ṣaḍdanta Jātaka* (No. 514 in Fausbøll's *The Jātaka*).
- **Sanskrit Buddhist Texts:** *Kalpadrumāvadāna*, *Jātakamālā* of Āryaśūra (parallel moral discourses), and the *Mahāvastu*.
- **Narrative Synopsis & Theological Meaning:**
  - King Ṣaḍdanta (Pali: Chaddanta) is a magnificent six-tusked white elephant (an incarnation of the Bodhisattva) who presides over an idyllic herd of 8,000 elephants in the Himalayan forest near the Golden Mountain and Lake Chaddanta.
  - He has two consorts: Mahāsubhaddā and Chullasubhaddā.
  - During a royal procession, the Bodhisattva shakes a blooming Śāla / Lotus tree. Flowers, fragrant pollen, and cool leaves fall upon Mahāsubhaddā, while dry twigs, dead leaves, and red ants fall upon Chullasubhaddā. Chullasubhaddā misunderstands this innocent accident as intentional neglect, harbor bitter jealousy, and performs extreme penance to be reborn as Queen of Benares to seek vengeance.
  - Reborn as Queen Subhaddā, chief consort of King Brahmadatta of Kāśī, she feigns a deadly pregnancy longing (*dohada*) and commands the hunter Sonuttara to trek to the remote Himalayas and sever the six tusks.
  - Sonuttara arrives, hides in a dug-out pit, and wears the ochre robe of a Buddhist monk (*kāṣāya*). When the Bodhisattva approaches, reverence for the monastic cloth restrains him from crushing the hunter.
  - When Sonuttara confesses his mission, the Bodhisattva, realizing the futility of worldly form and embodying the highest perfection of charity (*dāna-pāramitā*) and forbearance (*kṣānti-pāramitā*), assists the hunter by holding down his own tusks with his trunk as they are sawn off.
  - Sonuttara brings the six gleaming tusks to the palace in Benares. The Queen, gazing upon the tusks and hearing of the Bodhisattva's unfathomable generosity and compassion, is overcome by unbearable remorse (*paścāttāpa*), collapses into the King's lap, and dies of a broken heart.

### 1.3 Art Historical Identification at Cave 17
- **Mural Location:** Right aisle wall (between the third cell door and the rear corner), covering approximately 4.5 meters in width and 2.5 meters in height.
- **Compositional Strategy:** Continuous narrative (simultaneous representation of temporal sequences in an uninterrupted pictorial field without rigid panel borders). Figures move seamlessly across architectural pavilions, rocky grottos, and blooming lotus pools.
- **Key Reference Scholars:**
  - Dieter Schlingloff, *Studies in the Ajanta Paintings: Identifications and Interpretations* (1987), pp. 29–48. Schlingloff provides precise spatial reconstruction of the 8 narrative episodes.
  - Ghulam Yazdani, *Ajanta: The Colour and Monochrome Reproductions*, Vol. IV (1955), Plates XII–XVI.
  - Walter Spink, *Ajanta: History and Development, Volume 5: Cave by Cave* (Brill, 2007).
  - Vidya Dehejia, *Discourse in Early Buddhist Art: Visual Narratives of India* (Munshiram Manoharlal, 1997).

## SECTION 2: MAHABALIPURAM (MĀMALLAPURAM) THEMATIC SCULPTURE ANALYSIS

### 2.1 Historical & Dynastic Context
- **Dynasty:** Pallavas of Kanchipuram.
- **Key Monarchs & Chronology:**
  - Mahendravarman I (c. 600–630 CE): Initiated rock-cut architecture in Tamil Nadu (*vichitrachitta* / curious-minded). Inscribed Mandagapattu: "This brickless, timberless, metal-less, and mortar-less temple was caused to be made by King Vichitrachitta for Brahma, Ishvara, and Vishnu."
  - Narasimhavarman I Māmalla ("Great Wrestler", c. 630–668 CE): Subjugated the Western Chalukyas, sacked Vatapi (642 CE); founded port city of Māmallapuram; commissioned the Great Bas-Relief and the Pancha Rathas.
  - Parameśvaravarman I (c. 670–700 CE): Continued structural and rock-cut works.
  - Narasimhavarman II Rājasiṃha (c. 700–728 CE): Built the Shore Temple at Mahabalipuram and the Kailasanatha Temple at Kanchipuram; transitioned from monolithic rock-cut excavations to grand structural masonry temples in fine granite.
- **Geographic & Maritime Role:** Primary sea port of the Pallava empire, trading with Sri Lanka, Srivijaya (Sumatra/Java), Funan/Chenla (Cambodia), and the Roman Empire/Byzantium. Mentioned by Periplus (as *Sopatma*) and Ptolemy.

### 2.2 Monument 1: The Great Bas-Relief (Arjuna's Penance / Descent of the Ganges)
- **Physical Specifications:** Carved across two gigantic natural granite boulders measuring 27 meters (90 feet) in length and 9 meters (30 feet) in height, separated by a natural vertical fissure.
- **The Central Fissure:** Represents the cosmic descent of the River Gaṅgā from Shiva's matted tresses down to the mortal realm. Cisterns carved on top of the boulder gathered rainwater that was directed down the fissure through a stone sluice during festivals, creating a literal cascading waterfall over the sculpted Nāgarāja and Nāgī.
- **The Scholarly Debate & The Theory of *Śleṣa*:**
  - *Descent of the Ganges (Bhagīratha's Penance):* Supported by J. Ph. Vogel (1914), T. N. Ramachandran (1951), and ASI. Focuses on the penance of King Bhagīratha to redeem his 60,000 ancestors (Sons of Sagara); the river descending past celestials, hermits, and aquatic creatures; Shiva standing with right hand in *vara-mudrā* granting the wish; and the presence of the miniature temple of Vishnu/Varāha on the riverbank.
  - *Arjuna's Penance (Kirātārjunīya):* Supported by J. Dubreuil (1916) and C. Sivaramamurti (1978). Identifies the emaciated ascetic as Arjuna performing tapas on Indrakīla mountain to obtain the divine weapon *Pāśupatāstra* from Shiva for the Kurukshetra war.
  - *Unified Scholarly Consensus — Śleṣa (Polysemy):* Advanced by Michael D. Rabe (*The Great Penance at Māmallapuram: Deciphering a Visual Text*, 2001) and K. R. Srinivasan (*Cave-Temples of the Pallavas*, 1964). Rabe demonstrates that 7th-century Sanskrit court poetry celebrated *śleṣa* (simultaneous double meaning). The Pallava monarch is cast in the royal guise of both Arjuna (martial supremacy, obtaining invincible weapons) and Bhagīratha (pious king bringing life-giving water and prosperity to drought-prone Tondaimandalam).
- **Iconographic Details:**
  - The ascetic standing on one leg with arms raised above his head in *ūrdhva-bāhu* posture.
  - Four-armed Shiva accompanied by ganas (dwarfish attendants), holding trident (*triśūla*) and granting boons.
  - Over 140 individual relief carvings: gandharvas, kinnaras (half-human half-bird musicians), celestial vidyādharas soaring weightlessly toward the cleft.
  - Life-sized herd of elephants: majestic tusker protecting young calves underneath, praised as the pinnacle of naturalistic Indian animal sculpture.
  - The "Faux Ascetic Cat" (satirical fable from Pañcatantra): A cat standing on hind legs in imitation of the penance, while unsuspecting mice gambol peacefully around its feet, providing comic relief and philosophical balance against hypocritical asceticism.

### 2.3 Monument 2: The Shore Temple (Alaivāy-k-koyil)
- **Type:** Structural masonry temple constructed of dressed blocks of local granite (not rock-cut).
- **Date & Patron:** Reign of Narasimhavarman II Rājasiṃha (c. 700–728 CE).
- **Architecture & Shrines:**
  - Triple sanctum arrangement:
    1. East-facing Shiva shrine (*Kṣatriyasiṃheśvara*): Multi-tiered Dravidian vimāna (five storeys / *pañcatāla*), crowned by an octagonal śikhara, housing a faceted Dharalinga and a Somāskanda relief panel on the rear wall.
    2. West-facing Shiva shrine (*Rājasimheśvara*): Smaller three-storeyed vimāna facing the landward approach.
    3. Intermediate flat-roofed chamber: Houses a rock-cut monolithic sculpture of Vishnu reclining on Śeṣanāga (*Jalaśayana Perumāl* / *Anantaśāyī Vishnu*), deliberately carved in situ from an existing granite reef, showing early syncretism between Shaivism and Vaishnavism.
  - Surrounding enclosure: Parapet wall decorated with rows of couchant Nandi bulls.
- **Maritime Heritage & Conservation:**
  - Positioned directly on the Bay of Bengal surf, functioning historically as a visual landmark for mariners entering the harbor of Mamallapuram.
  - Severe environmental threats: Sea spray salt aerosol, salt crystallization inside granite pores, sand abrasion. Mitigated by ASI through stone desalting poultices and a buffer of Casuarina trees.
  - The 2004 Indian Ocean Tsunami: Receding water briefly uncovered collapsed masonry foundations, carved granite blocks, and a monolithic lion relief, confirming long-standing local traditions of submerged temples ("Seven Pagodas").

### 2.4 Monument 3: The Pancha Rathas (Five Chariots)
- **Monolithic Concept:** Five free-standing monolithic temples carved out of a single north-to-south sloping granite outcrop during the reign of Narasimhavarman I Māmalla (c. 630–668 CE). Unfinished due to dynastic wars and the king's death.
- **Architectural Typology (The "Laboratory of Dravidian Architecture"):**
  1. *Draupadi Ratha:* Square plan, curvilinear four-sided roof resembling a rural thatched hut (*kuṭāgāra* / *bānglā*). Dedicated to goddess Durgā; carved stone lion outside; interior sanctum shows Durgā with devotees offering flowers and one devotee offering his own head (*navakhandam* / self-sacrifice).
  2. *Arjuna Ratha:* Shares a contiguous plinth with Draupadi Ratha. Square two-storeyed (*dvitāla*) vimāna with pillared mukha-mandapa, crowned by an octagonal dome. Corner niches depict graceful royal couples (*mithunas*), Shiva with Nandi, and Vishnu with Garuda.
  3. *Bhima Ratha:* Oblong rectangular plan with an exquisite barrel-vaulted or wagon-shaped roof (*śālā-śikhara*), featuring chaitya arches (*kūḍu* motifs) along the cornice. Intended for a reclining Vishnu image.
  4. *Dharmaraja Ratha:* The southernmost, grandest, and most architecturally complete ratha. Three-tiered (*tritāla*) square vimāna rising to 35 feet, with an octagonal dome. Contains 28 exquisite sculptural niches, including the earliest identified depiction of *Ardhanārīśvara* (half-Shiva, half-Parvati) in Tamil art, *Harihara*, and portrait sculptures of King Narasimhavarman I bearing royal titles: *Śrī Megha* (Cloud of Prosperity) and *Trailokyanātha* (Lord of the Three Worlds).
  5. *Nakula-Sahadeva Ratha:* Situated slightly to the west; features an apsidal ground plan and an elephant-backed barrel roof (*gajapṛṣṭha* / *hasti-pṛṣṭha*), standing adjacent to a life-sized monolithic granite elephant sculpture.

### 2.5 Monument 4: Varaha Cave Temple (Varāha-maṇḍapa / Ādi-Varāha)
- **Type:** Rock-cut cave sanctuary excavated into the main granite hill of Mamallapuram during the mid-7th century (reign of Narasimhavarman I Māmalla).
- **Architectural Layout:** Low pillared portico (*mukha-maṇḍapa*) supported by two cushion-capital columns resting on the heads of seated yālis (vyāla/mythical horned lions), flanked by two pilasters. Leads into a transverse hall and a central rectangular shrine chamber.
- **Four Masterpiece Bas-Relief Panels:**
  1. *Bhū-Varāha (Varāha lifting the Earth Goddess):* Monumental portrayal of Vishnu's boar incarnation stepping with his right foot upon the hood of the serpent king Śeṣanāga, gently balancing the delicate figure of Bhūdevī on his right knee and forearm. The composition conveys profound tender intimacy combined with cosmic power.
  2. *Gaja-Lakṣmī (The Royal Bath of Fortune):* Goddess Lakṣmī seated upon an open lotus blossom, serenely poised with lotuses in hand, while two celestial elephants shower water over her from upturned jars, attended by celestial apsaras carrying fragrant ointments and flowers.
  3. *Trivikrama (Vāmana's Cosmic Strides):* Vishnu expands into cosmic proportions, with one leg planted firmly on earth and the other thrust dramatically into the celestial sphere of Brahma, flanked by Sun, Moon, and celestial witnesses.
  4. *Durgā / Harihara:* Flanked by royal devotees and attendants, showcasing the refined, elongated anatomical proportions and high-relief carving characteristic of classic Pallava art.

## SECTION 3: KOLAM & RANGOLI — SACRED GEOMETRY, CULTURE & ETHNOMATHEMATICS

### 3.1 Cultural Anthropology & The Essential Distinction
- **The Core Distinction:**
  - *Kolam* and *Rangoli* belong to the broader Indic tradition of sacred threshold floor art, but they represent distinct regional paradigms, material chemistries, and geometric philosophies:
  - **Kolam (Tamil Nadu & Dravidian South):**
    - Medium: Fine rice flour (*arisi maavu*), either applied dry between the thumb and forefinger or as a wet ground paste (*padi kolam* or *kavi kolam* edged with red ochre *kāvi*).
    - Daily Rhythm: Created fresh every morning before sunrise after washing the threshold (*vāsal*) with water and cow dung solution (which acts as a natural antiseptic).
    - Ecological Ethos: Conceived as *bhūta yajña* (one of the five daily sacrificial duties in Hindu ethics)—nourishing ants, beetles, birds, and soil micro-organisms with edible rice flour, thus turning art into an act of living ecological charity (Vijaya Nagarajan, *Feeding a Thousand Souls*, 2018).
    - Mathematical Matrix: Predominantly topological, based on a rigorous orthogonal or staggered dot-matrix (*pulli*), closed curves (*kambi* / *sikku*), and endless interlacing knots (*Brahma Mudi*).
  - **Rangoli (Maharashtra, Gujarat, North & Central India):**
    - Etymology: Sanskrit *Rangavalli* (colored creepers / row of colors).
    - Medium: Colored powders (coarse stone powder mixed with bright mineral pigments or food-safe dyes), flowers, grains, and sand.
    - Timing: Primarily festive and ceremonial (Diwali, weddings, harvest celebrations).
    - Visual Language: Figurative, floral, and symbolic motifs: sacred lamps (*diya*), peacocks, swastikas, footprint of goddess Lakshmi, kalasha pots, and expansive freehand gradients.
  - **Pan-Indian Regional Matrix:**
    - *Muggu* (Andhra Pradesh & Telangana): Drawn with chalk powder or rice flour; intricate grid patterns (*Chitti Muggu*), drawn especially during Sankranti with yellow pumpkin flowers (*gobbemma*).
    - *Alpana* (Bengal): Fluid, freehand floor art drawn with wet rice paste (*pituli*) using a small cotton cloth, featuring flowing floral creepers, conch shells (*shankha*), and stylized footprints of Lakshmi during Kojagari Lakshmi Puja.
    - *Jhoti / Chita* (Odisha): Traditional white rice paste art executed on walls and floors during Manabasa Gurubara, featuring stylized paddy ears, lotus petals, and feet of Goddess Lakshmi.
    - *Aripana* (Mithila / Bihar): Floor art made during lifecycle rituals (samskaras) using crushed rice paste, vermilion, and kajal, rich in Tantric cosmograms and fertility symbols.
    - *Chowkpurana* (Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, Madhya Pradesh): Geometric floor patterns using wheat flour, turmeric, and dry plaster, setting the sacred precinct for domestic rituals and weddings.

### 3.2 Ethnomathematics & Computer Science Analysis
- **Pioneering Computational Studies (Gift Siromoney et al., 1974–1984):**
  - Prof. Gift Siromoney, Rani Siromoney, and Kamala Krithivasan at the Department of Statistics, Madras Christian College, were the first to formalize Kolam using formal language theory and theoretical computer science.
  - Demonstrated that Kolam patterns can be described as **Array Grammars** and **Picture Languages** capable of generating recursive, infinite geometrical families.
- **Grid Topologies (Pulli Matrix):**
  1. *Nēr Pulli (Orthogonal / Square Grid):* Dots arranged in a Cartesian $m \times n$ matrix where each dot aligns horizontally and vertically.
  2. *Kanni Pulli / Sandhu Pulli (Staggered / Interleaved Grid):* Dots in each successive row placed in the equilateral interstitial gaps between the dots of the preceding row (e.g., diamond grid sequence: $1-3-5-7-5-3-1$ or $15 \to 1$).
- **Topological Invariants & Knot Theory:**
  - *Sikku Kolam (Looped / Labyrinthine Kolam):* The practitioner draws continuous closed loops that encircle the dots without touching them, navigating according to strict parity rules.
  - *Brahma Mudi (Brahma's Knot):* A single, unbroken, closed curve traversing all dots in a symmetric pattern. Topologically equivalent to an **Eulerian Circuit** in graph theory. Symbolizes the cosmic cycle of creation, preservation, and dissolution (*samsāra*) with neither beginning nor end.
  - *Symmetry Groups:* Kolam patterns embody formal point groups:
    - $C_4$ and $D_4$ (4-fold cyclic and dihedral symmetry).
    - $C_8$ and $D_8$ (8-fold octagonal symmetry, common in lotus kolams).
    - Reflectional symmetry across vertical, horizontal, and diagonal axes.
- **Fractals and Self-Similarity:**
  - Marcia Ascher (*Ethnomathematics*, 1991) and Yanagisawa (2007) analyzed how classical Kolams exhibit fractal dimensions, scaling self-similarity, and recursive branching akin to Sierpiński carpets and Peano space-filling curves.
