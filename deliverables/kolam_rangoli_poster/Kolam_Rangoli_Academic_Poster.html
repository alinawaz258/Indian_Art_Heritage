1. The Living Ritual: Bhūta Yajña & Threshold

Across South Asia, domestic thresholds (vāsal in Tamil, dehlī in Hindi) represent dynamic liminal boundaries separating the chaotic exterior world from the sanctified domestic interior. Every morning before dawn, women sweep and sprinkle the earth with water or cow-dung solution, creating a fresh canvas for sacred geometry.

Crucially, traditional Kolam is an act of Bhūta Yajña—one of the five daily duties in classical Hindu ethics signifying charity to non-human beings. By using raw, edible rice flour (arisi maavu), the practitioner feeds thousands of ants, birds, and soil microbes, transforming ritual art into a living, daily communion with urban biodiversity (Nagarajan, 2018).

2. The Essential Distinction: Kolam vs. Rangoli

Popular discourse often erroneously conflates Kolam and Rangoli as interchangeable decorative patterns. Ethnomusicologists and anthropologists document fundamental divergences in medium, temporality, and geometry:

Criterion Kolam (Tamil Nadu) Rangoli (North/West India)
Primary Medium Coarse edible rice flour (arisi maavu); red ochre border (kāvi) Colored synthetic/mineral powders, sawdust, sand, flower petals
Temporality Executed daily at dawn at residential thresholds Predominantly festive (Diwali, Makar Sankranti, weddings)
Geometric Logic Rigid dot-grid matrix (pulli), continuous loops (sikku) Pictorial, floral, and symbolic motifs (diyas, peacocks, kalash)
Ethical Ethos Bhūta Yajña (feeding living organisms) Śobhā (festive ornamentation & auspicious display)

3. Topologies of the Dot Grid (Pulli)

Kolam patterns are built upon rigorous topological frameworks known as Pulli (dots). The artist lays down coordinate matrices that dictate path navigation:

Figure 2. Grid Typologies: (Left) Nēr Pulli: Orthogonal $m \times n$ Cartesian grid. (Right) Kanni Pulli: Interleaved equilateral diamond lattice ($1 \to 5 \to 1$).

1. Nēr Pulli (Straight Grid): Dots aligned in Cartesian rows and columns ($x_i, y_j$).
2. Kanni / Sandhu Pulli (Staggered Grid): Dots placed in the interstitial hollows of preceding rows, forming hexagonal close-packing lattices.

4. Knot Theory & Eulerian Loops (Brahma Mudi)

In Sikku (looped) Kolam, lines do not connect dots; rather, a continuous line curves around the dots without crossing them illegally or intersecting itself haphazardly.

Figure 3. Topological Eulerian Circuit (Brahma Mudi): An unbroken closed loop tracing all nodes with zero beginning or end.

Topologically, the revered Brahma Mudi (Brahma's Knot) forms an Eulerian circuit in graph theory—a closed path that visits every coordinate node exactly under strict symmetry invariants. This unbroken cycle symbolizes saṃsāra: the endless cosmic cycle of generation, preservation, and dissolution.

5. Array Grammars & Computer Science

Pioneering research by Prof. Gift Siromoney (Madras Christian College, 1974) demonstrated that Kolam constitutes a formal Picture Language. Using Array Grammars, Kolam designs can be generated algorithmically by recursive rewrite rules, inspiring modern research in robotic path-planning, DNA self-assembly, and space-filling Peano curves (Ascher, 1991).

6. Pan-Indian Regional Diversity Matrix

Floor art exists across the subcontinent in distinct regional avatars:

  • Muggu (Andhra & Telangana): Chalk/rice powder; intricate grids drawn with yellow pumpkin flowers during Sankranti.
  • Alpana (West Bengal): Fluid, lyrical strokes with wet fermented rice paste (pituli) for Kojagari Lakshmi Puja.
  • Jhoti / Chita (Odisha): Stylized footprints of Lakshmi and paddy ears for Manabasa Gurubara.
  • Aripana (Mithila / Bihar): Tantric cosmograms and fertility symbols using rice paste, vermilion, and kajal.
  • Chowkpurana (North India): Sacred geometric squares in flour and turmeric for domestic sacraments.

7. Symmetry Groups ($D_4, D_8$) & Fractals

Figure 5. Point Groups: Dihedral $D_4$ symmetry and recursive fractal subdivisions.

Kolams embody point groups $C_4, D_4, C_8, D_8$, exhibiting 90°/45° rotational symmetry and horizontal/vertical/diagonal reflection axes. Advanced designs exhibit self-similarity akin to Sierpiński fractals.

8. Conclusion: Sacred Math as Living Heritage

Far from mere decorative handicraft, Kolam and Rangoli represent sophisticated systems of indigenous knowledge where mathematics, ecological ethics, and aesthetic beauty coalesce. Handed down across generations of women, this ephemeral threshold art proves that sacred geometry lives not only in stone monuments, but in the morning gestures of domestic devotion.
