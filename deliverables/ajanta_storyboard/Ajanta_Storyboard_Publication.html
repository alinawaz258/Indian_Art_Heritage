The Murals of Cave 17 & The Theological Power of the Ṣaḍdanta Tale

Carved into the sheer basalt horseshoe gorge of the Waghora River during the golden age of the Vākāṭaka dynasty (c. 460–477 CE), Cave 17 at Ajanta preserves some of the most emotionally nuanced and technically sophisticated wall-paintings surviving from the ancient world. Commissioned by Rājā Upendragupta, a pious feudatory ruler of the province of Rṣīka under the suzerainty of the Vākāṭaka emperor Hariṣeṇa, Cave 17 was conceived not merely as a monastic dormitory (vihāra), but as an expansive visual textbook of Buddhist ethics. As celebrated in verse 29 of the cave's 29-verse Sanskrit donor inscription, the monastery was excavated so that "as long as the sun dispels darkness with its rays, so long may this mountain sanctuary grant enlightenment to the world."

Unlike true Italian fresco buono, where pigments are painted into damp lime plaster, the Ajanta murals were executed using a refined tempera / fresco-secco technique. The basalt walls were first coated with a coarse mud plaster comprising ferruginous clay, rock grit, sand, organic plant fibres, and cow dung. Over this dry underlayer, artists applied a smooth, 1–3 mm slip of fine slaked lime (chunam) or kaolin, burnished with an agate stone. The master painters worked with natural inorganic earth pigments: red and yellow ochres (iron oxides), celadonite green (terra verde), lampblack, lime white, and crucially, precious lapis lazuli imported from the Badakhshan mines of modern Afghanistan, confirming the cosmopolitan mercantile networks of 5th-century India.

Among the narrative cycles decorating Cave 17's right aisle wall, the Ṣaḍdanta Jātaka (Pali Jātaka No. 514) stands as the supreme emotional climax. As demonstrated in Dieter Schlingloff's authoritative spatial reconstructions (1987), the mural eschews rigid compartmentalized borders in favor of continuous narrative: figures transition seamlessly from tranquil lotus pools into royal palace pavilions and rugged mountain crags. The story dramatizes the tragic genesis of jealousy, the betrayal of sacred monastic vestments, the Bodhisattva's boundless sacrifice of his own body (dāna-pāramitā), and the catastrophic nemesis of remorse.

The Illustrated Storyboard (8 Sequential Panels)

PANEL 1 OF 8
The Himalayan Lotus Sanctuary
Padmavane Chaddantavāsa
Artistic Reconstruction — Inspired by Cave 17 Right Wall
"In ancient times, the Bodhisattva dwelt in the secluded Himalayan vales as Ṣaḍdanta, the noble king of eight thousand elephants. Luminous as snow, endowed with six radiant tusks, he governed his realm with supreme gentleness amidst azure lotuses and fragrance-laden groves."
King Ṣaḍdanta: "Drink freely of the pure waters, gentle ones. In this secluded sanctuary, compassion is our sole law and sanctuary."
Queen Mahāsubhaddā: "The lake is blessed by your presence, Lord. May peace ever shelter this herd."
Iconographic & Scholarly Notes: Dieter Schlingloff Scene 1 on Cave 17 right corridor wall. Notice the naturalistic rendering of elephants frolicking amidst blue utpala lotuses painted with imported lapis lazuli. The six tusks symbolize the mastery of the six paramitas (perfections).
PANEL 2 OF 8
The Slight of the Śāla Blossom & The Fatal Vow
Subhaddāyāḥ Krodhapranidhi
Artistic Reconstruction — Inspired by Cave 17 Right Wall
"A sudden misfortune ignited tragic wrath. As the king shook a flowering bough, blossoms graced the elder queen, while twigs and biting insects fell upon Chullasubhaddā. Consumed by poisonous jealousy, she mistook chance for deliberate scorn."
Chullasubhaddā (aside): "He honors her before the herd and casts refuse upon me! If penance has virtue, I vow to perish and be reborn as queen in Benares to destroy him!"
King Ṣaḍdanta (in distance): "Come, Subhaddā, the fragrant grove awaits us all..."
Iconographic & Scholarly Notes: Cave 17 continuous mural; Pali Jātaka 514; Schlingloff Scene 2. Chullasubhaddā's averted posture in the shadowy grove conveys psychological isolation and the tragic genesis of karmic retribution (saṃsāra).
PANEL 3 OF 8
The Royal Court of Benares & The Feigned Longing
Vārāṇasyām Dohadaśchalam
Artistic Reconstruction — Inspired by Cave 17 Right Wall
"Her bitter prayer was granted. Reborn in the royal house of Kāśī as the beloved queen of King Brahmadatta, the memory of past grievance burned unquenched. Feigning a fatal pregnancy longing, she summoned the realm's fiercest hunters."
Queen Subhaddā: "O King! In my dreams, six radiant tusks of pure crystal light appeared. Unless their brilliance touches my breast, your queen shall not survive the dawn!"
King Brahmadatta: "Rest, my beloved. Whatever exists beneath the vault of heaven, my hunters shall fetch for your solace."
Iconographic & Scholarly Notes: Cave 17 upper register. Masterpiece of Vākāṭaka courtly depiction: slender pillars, pearl necklaces (ekāvalī), striped cotton textiles, and expressive court maidens holding fly-whisks.
PANEL 4 OF 8
The Deceit of the Ochre Robe
Kāṣāyachhadmena Sonuttara
Artistic Reconstruction — Inspired by Cave 17 Right Wall
"For seven years, seven months, and seven days, the hunter Sonuttara traversed jagged crags. Knowing the Bodhisattva's boundless devotion to truth, he wrapped himself in the holy yellow robe of a renunciant and hid within a pit, aiming his poisoned shaft."
Hunter Sonuttara (aside): "Even the mightiest beast bows before the robe of the Buddha. In this guise, my arrow cannot fail."
King Ṣaḍdanta (approaching): "Who rests within the pit in the garb of peace?"
Iconographic & Scholarly Notes: Central thematic pivot in Buddhist ethics. The mural emphasizes the sacrilege of wearing the kāṣāya robe for deceit, contrasting Sonuttara's predatory crouch with the Bodhisattva's towering calm.
PANEL 5 OF 8
The Supreme Gift (Dāna Pāramitā)
Dānasya Parā Kāṣṭhā
Artistic Reconstruction — Inspired by Cave 17 Right Wall
"Though struck by venom, the Bodhisattva quelled all wrath at the sight of the monastic robe. Hearing of the Queen's command, he knelt with boundless grace. Finding the hunter too weak to saw the ivory, the Great Being grasped the blade with his own trunk, completing the sacrifice of his own flesh."
King Ṣaḍdanta: "Take these tusks, brother, not out of malice, but that your journey not be in vain. Sweeter to me than these ivory shafts is the omniscience of supreme enlightenment (bodhi)!"
Hunter Sonuttara: "O Great King... forgive this wretched hand that strikes a living god!"
Iconographic & Scholarly Notes: Cave 17 right wall; central dramatic climax; the pinnacle of dāna-pāramitā (the perfection of giving) and kṣānti (forbearance). Schlingloff highlights the tender downward curve of Ṣaḍdanta's trunk.
PANEL 6 OF 8
The Return of the Severed Tusks
Rājasabhāyāṃ Dantapradānam
Artistic Reconstruction — Inspired by Cave 17 Right Wall
"Bearing the sacred burden through forest and mountain, Sonuttara returned to the capital of Kāśī. He laid the six miraculous tusks before the throne—silent, incorruptible witnesses to a sacrifice beyond mortal comprehension."
Hunter Sonuttara: "Behold, O Queen! The six tusks of the Great White King. Know that he surrendered them willingly, offering blessings even upon those who sought his life."
Queen Subhaddā: "He... he gave them willingly?"
Iconographic & Scholarly Notes: Cave 17, lower court pavilion. The tusks radiate a supernatural pearlescent glow against the rich mineral ochre background of the royal hall.
PANEL 7 OF 8
The Swoon of Remorse & Death
Subhaddāyāḥ Paścāttāpaḥ Prāṇatyāgaśca
Artistic Reconstruction — Inspired by Cave 17 Right Wall
"Touching the ivory, the full horror of her deeds pierced her heart like a thunderbolt. The veil of delusion dissolved: she remembered the noble king who had loved her, whose only fault was her own bitter envy. Overwhelmed by unbearable grief and remorse (paścāttāpa), her heart broke, and she fell lifeless upon the palace floor."
Queen Subhaddā (final whisper): "My Lord... what have I destroyed? Your love was as pure as the lotus, and my envy was as black as night!"
King Brahmadatta: "Subhaddā! Awaken! What sorrow has stolen your breath?"
Iconographic & Scholarly Notes: Cave 17, dramatic lower right scene. One of the most famous human figure studies in ancient art: the swooning queen caught by her attendants, celebrated by Yazdani and Kramrisch for its psychological realism.
PANEL 8 OF 8
Upasaṃhāra: The Eternal Moral
Upasaṃhāraḥ: Śāśvata Dharmaḥ
Artistic Reconstruction — Inspired by Cave 17 Right Wall
"Thus taught the Master: 'Envy consumes the vessel that contains it, but compassion endures across countless aeons.' King Brahmadatta enshrined the sacred tusks, while across the mountains, the tale of Ṣaḍdanta was carved and painted by devoted hands on the basalt cliffs of Ajanta for generations yet unborn."
Narrator / Chronicler: "Hatred does not cease by hatred at any time; hatred ceases only by love. This is an eternal law." (Dhammapada 5)
Cave 17 Inscription (v. 29): "May this mountain vihāra grant delight and release to all living beings!"
Iconographic & Scholarly Notes: Synthesizes the moral doctrine of the Jātaka with the architecture of Cave 17. The mural served as an immersive visual sermon for monks and pilgrims meditating along the ambulatory.
